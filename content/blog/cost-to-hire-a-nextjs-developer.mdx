## How much does a Next.js developer charge?

It depends on whether you're buying hours or a result. Search for **next.js developer rates** and you'll mostly find staffing companies listing hourly prices by country and seniority. Those numbers are real for what they sell: a developer's time, often full time, for a product team.

A business that wants one website or store is buying something else: a finished project. For that, a fixed price per package is easier to compare, because it tells you what you get and what it costs before anyone starts.

That's how I quote. The prices in the table are starting points. The final price depends on the features you need, and you get it in writing after a free call, before you pay anything.

The same goes for **react developer cost**. Next.js is a framework built on React, and the [Next.js documentation](https://nextjs.org/docs) shows what it adds: pages rendered before they reach the browser, routing and image handling. For a business site the framework barely changes the price; the pages and features do.

## Is fixed price or hourly better for a website?

For a small business website, fixed price is usually better. Here's the **fixed price vs hourly developer** trade-off in plain terms:

| | Fixed price | Hourly |
| --- | --- | --- |
| You know the total | Before you start | Only at the end |
| If the work takes longer | The developer absorbs it | You pay for it |
| Needs a clear scope | Yes, written down | No |
| Changes mid-project | Priced before they're done | Added to the hours |
| Best for | A defined site or store | Open-ended work, ongoing tasks |

Hourly makes sense when nobody can define the work yet, such as a long-running product with changing priorities. A business website rarely looks like that. You know the pages, you know roughly what the store needs, so the scope can be written down and priced once.

The catch with fixed price is the scope. It has to list the pages and features, and what isn't included. A vague scope turns a fixed price into an argument later.

## Cost to hire a Next.js developer: which package fits you?

Most people I talk to know what they need once it's put this way:

- **You need to be found and contacted.** A service business, a consultant or a portfolio needs pages that explain what you do and a clear way to reach you. That's the landing page / portfolio package, from $400, and it's the one most often grown with an extra page or two.
- **You sell products and take orders.** You need a shop, a cart, a checkout and a place to manage orders and stock. That's the ecommerce store with admin dashboard, from $1,000, and guest checkout is enough for most new stores.
- **Customers need their own accounts.** If shoppers should sign in to see past orders, save details or reorder, it's the full ecommerce app, from $2,000.
- **Your team needs a tool, not a shop window.** Screens for staff to manage records, with roles and reports, are a custom dashboard (from $1,200) or a custom web app (from $2,500), quoted by features.

If two of these sound right, start with the smaller one. A site built in custom code can usually grow into the bigger package later, so ask on the call what that step would involve.

- **Extra pages:** $50 each beyond the package's limit.
- **Content editing (CMS):** +$150, so you can change text and images yourself.
- **A second language:** +$250.

## What costs come after launch?

The developer's price is one bill. These are billed by other services, usually monthly or yearly:

| Cost | Who bills it | In the developer's price? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Design, build, launch SEO setup | The developer | Yes |
| Bug fixes in the first 30 days | The developer | Yes, with me |
| Hosting | The hosting provider, such as Vercel | No |
| Database | The database provider | No |
| Domain name | Your domain registrar | No |
| Ongoing maintenance and new features | The developer | No, quoted separately |

A small site often starts on a host's entry plan; check the current plans on [Vercel's pricing page](https://vercel.com/pricing) rather than trusting a figure in an article. Agree in writing who holds each account, who pays each bill and what a handover includes.

## How do you compare two developer quotes?

Two quotes with different totals often describe different projects. Before you compare prices, check:

- [ ] **Pages:** how many are included, and what does each extra page cost?
- [ ] **Features:** is the admin dashboard, the login system or the content editing listed by name?
- [ ] **Delivery time:** in days or weeks, written down.
- [ ] **Revisions:** how many rounds are included?
- [ ] **Support:** how long are bugs fixed for free after launch?
- [ ] **Payment terms:** how much upfront, and what triggers the rest?
- [ ] **Running costs:** does the quote say which bills you'll pay to other services?
