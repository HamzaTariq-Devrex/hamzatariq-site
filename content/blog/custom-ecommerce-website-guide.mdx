A custom ecommerce website is an online store built in code around how you sell, instead of a template on a store platform. You get your own design, a storefront that loads fast and an admin dashboard to manage products and orders. With me, a custom store with an admin dashboard starts at $1,000, includes up to 10 pages and unlimited products, and usually takes 2–3 weeks.

It's the right choice when your products, ordering or brand don't fit a template. It's the wrong choice if you need to launch this week with no budget for anyone to look after it.

**A custom store usually includes:**

| Part | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| Storefront | Home, collections, product pages, cart and checkout, designed around your brand |
| Admin dashboard | Add products, change prices and stock, see and update orders |
| Database | Products, customers and orders, in an account you own |
| SEO setup | Pre-rendered pages, titles, descriptions, sitemap and product structured data |
| Ordering channels | Checkout, plus WhatsApp ordering or custom-order requests where you need them |

Below: how it compares with Shopify, what it costs, how long it takes, and what the stores I've built at Devrex Digital look like.

## What does a custom ecommerce website include?

"Custom" means every screen is written for your store. Nothing is a theme with your logo dropped in. In practice that covers three layers.

**The storefront.** This is what shoppers see: the home page, collection and category pages, filters, product pages, cart and checkout. Because it's built for you, it can present products the way your customers buy them. A wedding-wear label can show each collection like a lookbook. A jewellery store with a big catalogue can put bridal, kundan, earrings and necklaces one tap away.

**The admin dashboard.** This is where you run the store. More on it below, because it's the part you'll use most.

**The data layer.** Products, stock, customers and orders live in a database. On my builds that's Supabase: login, a Postgres database, image storage and access rules, so a customer can only ever see their own orders and only you can change prices.

A custom online store can also hold things a template can't do well: custom-order requests, made-to-measure options, a currency selector for buyers abroad, WhatsApp ordering next to the normal checkout, or sale campaigns that take over the home page for a weekend.

## Should I build a custom store or use Shopify?

This is the question most owners start with, so here's a straight answer, with one disclosure: I don't do Shopify work. No themes, no apps, no chatbots on Shopify stores. I build custom Next.js stores. That means I have a reason to prefer custom, so I'll tell you when I wouldn't.

| | Shopify | Custom store (Next.js) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Launch speed | Fastest: pick a theme and add products | Planned build; my store package is 2–3 weeks |
| Design | Within what the theme and apps allow | Anything you need, built around your brand |
| Monthly costs | Platform plan plus paid apps, which add up | Hosting and database, often on free or low tiers at small scale |
| Unusual ordering | Possible through apps and workarounds | Built in: custom orders, WhatsApp ordering, made-to-measure |
| Speed and SEO | Depends on the theme and how many apps load | Pre-rendered pages with SEO set up from launch |
| Ownership | You rent the platform | You own the code, database and accounts |
| Who maintains it | The platform, plus you | A developer for changes (30 days of fixes included with me) |

**Shopify is the better call when** you want to test a product quickly, your store is standard (products, cart, checkout and nothing unusual), or you have nobody to call when something needs changing.

**A custom ecommerce website is the better call when** the way you sell doesn't fit a template, your current store is slow and stuffed with apps, you want your monthly bills under your control, or you want to own the whole thing outright.

**Moving from Instagram, WhatsApp or another platform?** Your products, photos and prices come with you. On a move from another store platform, I keep the product links that already get traffic, or redirect them to the new ones, so search results and links you've shared still land on the right product. Customer lists and past orders can usually be exported and imported too; I check what your current platform allows on the free call, before you commit to anything.

If your real question is which platform suits a business site in general, not just a store, I compare the options in [Next.js vs WordPress for a business website](/blog/nextjs-vs-wordpress-for-business-website).

## How long does it take to build an online store?

My ecommerce store package usually takes 2–3 weeks of build time. A full ecommerce app depends on its features, so its timeline is planned on the free call.

What decides the timeline is rarely the code. It's how quickly the store's content is ready. The things that most often slow a store down:

- **Product photos and descriptions arriving late.** I can build the store with placeholders, but it can't launch without the real thing.
- **Undecided policies.** Delivery charges, returns and which countries you ship to change how checkout works. Decide them before the build starts.
- **New features mid-build.** Adding customer accounts halfway through turns a store into a full app. That's fine, but it changes the price and the date.
- **Slow feedback.** You get 2 revision rounds. Gathering everyone's comments into each round keeps things moving.

If you have products, photos, prices and policies ready on day one, the store package's timeline is realistic.

## How to build an ecommerce website, step by step

Here is how a build runs with me, from the first message to launch. The steps are the same whether you need a small store or a full app.

1. **Free call.** We go through what you sell, how customers order today, which pages you need and which features matter. You get a clear quote afterwards.
2. **Plan and design.** I map the pages and the path from landing on the site to placing an order, then design the store around your brand before any code is written.
3. **Build in custom code.** I build the storefront in Next.js and the admin dashboard on a Supabase database, with no templates. You review each part as it's ready.
4. **Add your content.** Products, photos, prices and policies go in through the admin dashboard, so you learn it while the store is still private.
5. **Test the money paths.** Every route to an order is tested on a phone: add to cart, change quantities, sold-out items, checkout and order confirmation.
6. **Launch and SEO setup.** The store goes live with pre-rendered pages, unique titles and descriptions, a sitemap and structured data, so Google can find every product.
7. **Free fixes.** For 30 days after launch, any bug I find or you report is fixed at no cost.

If you're planning to build an ecommerce website with someone else, the same steps make a good checklist for their proposal. A quote that skips step 5 or step 6 is missing the parts that cost you sales later.

## The ecommerce admin dashboard: what you'll do every day

Shoppers see the storefront once per visit. You'll live in the admin dashboard for years, so it deserves more attention than it usually gets in a quote.

On the stores I build, the ecommerce admin dashboard covers the jobs an owner does every day:

- **Add a product** with photos, price, sizes or variants, description and the collection it belongs to.
- **Change a price** or put a collection on sale for a weekend, then end the sale.
- **Mark stock.** Set quantities or mark an item sold out, so nobody can order something you can't send.
- **See new orders**, open one, and move it from new to packed to shipped.
- **Find a customer's order** when they message you asking where it is.

It's built in React on the same database as the store, so a change you save appears on the storefront straight away.

The dashboard is also where custom pays off. If you take made-to-measure orders, it can store each customer's measurements with their order. If you sell through WhatsApp as well, it can hold those orders next to the web orders. A template admin gives you its own idea of a store; a custom one follows yours.

## Store or full ecommerce app: which do you need?

The difference between my two ecommerce packages is customer accounts.

**The ecommerce store** ($1,000 and up) gives shoppers everything they need to buy: browsing, filters, product pages, cart and checkout as a guest. You get the full admin dashboard.

**The full ecommerce app** ($2,000 and up) adds customer logins, saved accounts and order history, and raises the page limit from 10 to 15. Customers can sign in, see past orders and reorder.

You probably need the full app if most of your sales come from repeat customers who reorder the same things, or if customers need to track several orders over time. You probably don't if most of your buyers order once or twice a year.

## Product pages Google can read

A store only earns from search if Google can read its products. This is where many template and single-page stores quietly lose: the product name, price and description are added by JavaScript after the page loads, so search engines see far less than shoppers do.

On a custom Next.js store, every product page is pre-rendered. The HTML Google downloads already holds the product name, price, photos and description. On top of that, each product gets:

- **Its own title and description**, written for that product, not one shared store title.
- **Product structured data**, the labelled code that tells Google the price and whether it's in stock. Google's guide to [product structured data](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/product) shows what it can display.
- **Optimised images.** Product photos are the heaviest thing on a store. Next.js [image optimization](https://nextjs.org/docs/app/getting-started/images) serves each one in a modern format at the right size, and only loads it when the shopper scrolls near it.
- **Clean links**, such as `/collections/bridal` and `/products/<product-name>`, with every product in the sitemap.

If you already run a React store that doesn't show up in search, the problem and its fixes are in my [React SEO guide](/blog/react-seo-guide).

## When is a custom store the wrong choice?

I'd rather lose a project than build a store that doesn't fit. A custom ecommerce website is probably not right for you if:

- **You need to launch this week.** A custom build takes weeks. A platform store can be live in days.
- **You're still testing whether anyone wants the product.** A few good photos and a WhatsApp number, or a simple platform store, can prove demand first.
- **You have no one to call for changes.** Custom code needs a developer for new features. If that's a problem, a platform you can change yourself is safer.
- **You want to stay on Shopify.** I don't build on it. Hire a Shopify specialist instead.
- **Your catalogue is tiny and never changes.** A landing page with an order form (from $400 with me) may be all you need.

## Who should own the store, and what to check before you hire?

Whoever builds your store, put these in your name from the first day: the domain, the hosting account, the database and every payment account. You should be able to export products, customers and orders without asking anyone. If a developer keeps any of them in their own name, leaving them later gets expensive. If your developer is in another country, my guide to [working with a remote web developer](/blog/working-with-a-remote-web-developer) covers payments, time zones and handover.

Before you hire anyone for ecommerce website development, ask to see a live store they built and test it yourself on your phone: place an order up to payment, find a sold-out product, and ask them to add a product in the admin while you watch. I list seven of these tests, none of which need coding knowledge, in my guide on [what to check when you hire a Next.js developer for ecommerce](/blog/hire-nextjs-developer-for-ecommerce).

## What to prepare before you contact a developer

Having these ready makes your quote accurate and your build fast. Tick off what you already have:

- [ ] A list of the pages you need (home, shop, collections, about, contact, policies).
- [ ] Your products in a spreadsheet: name, price, sizes or variants, description, collection.
- [ ] Product photos, ideally on a consistent background.
- [ ] Delivery charges, the countries you ship to, and your returns policy.
- [ ] How you'll take payment, and whether you also want WhatsApp ordering.
- [ ] Whether you need customer accounts now, or can add them later.
- [ ] Two or three stores you like, and what you like about each.
- [ ] Your domain, or the name you want to register.
