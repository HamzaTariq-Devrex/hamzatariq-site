A **custom web app for small business** is software built around how one business works, used in a web browser on a computer or phone, with logins for your team (and sometimes your customers) and your data in a real database. With me, a custom dashboard for your team starts from **$1,200** and takes 2–4 weeks; a custom web app with its own users and workflows starts from **$2,500** and takes 4–8 weeks.

It's worth building when your spreadsheets and ready-made tools start costing you hours or mistakes every week. It isn't worth building before that. This guide covers how to tell which side you're on, what the first version should include, what it costs, how long it takes and how to prepare.

## What is a custom web app for small business?

It's the screens your business runs on, built for you instead of rented from a generic tool. A few examples of what that looks like for a small business:

| What it does | Who logs in | Usually called |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Manage orders, products and stock in one place | You and your staff | Admin dashboard |
| Track customers, jobs or bookings and their status | You and your staff | Internal tool |
| Let customers sign up, order, upload or track something | Your team and your customers | Customer-facing web app |
| Show sales and the numbers you track as charts | You and your managers | Reporting dashboard |

What makes it "custom" is that the screens, the data and the rules come from your workflow. A ready-made tool gives every customer the same screens and asks you to adapt. A custom one starts from the question "what does your team do every day?" and builds exactly that.

"Web app" means it runs in the browser. There's nothing to install, the same build works on a laptop and a phone, and updates reach everyone the moment they go live. Modern web apps can even be added to a phone's home screen like an app; MDN's guide to [progressive web apps](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Progressive_web_apps) explains how that works.

Internal tools for small business are the most common kind I'm asked about: an admin dashboard where staff manage orders and products, see what needs doing and stop asking each other which file is current.

## When does a small business need a custom web app?

The signs are rarely dramatic. They show up as small, repeated costs that nobody adds up:

- **Nobody is sure which version is current.** The order list lives in one spreadsheet, the stock in another, and the latest changes are in a chat. Every decision starts with "let me check".
- **The same manual work, every day.** Someone copies orders from messages into a sheet, then from the sheet into an invoice, then into a delivery list. Each copy is a chance for a typo.
- **Your tools don't talk to each other.** You pay for several subscriptions and still rebuild the same report by hand at the end of the week.
- **Ready-made software makes you change how you work.** It forces a process that doesn't fit, charges for features you never touch and misses the one screen you need every day.
- **Too many people in one file.** Staff can see and edit everything, including things they shouldn't, because a spreadsheet can't say "this person only edits orders".
- **One person holds it all together.** The system works only because one person understands the formulas. When they're away, it stops.

If two or three of these sound familiar, a custom business web application will usually pay for itself in saved time and fewer mistakes. If only one does, fix that one problem first; a better spreadsheet template or a single ready-made tool might be enough.

## When should a small business not build one?

Most guides on this topic are written by people who sell software, so they skip this part. These are the situations where I tell people to wait:

- **Your whole process fits in one spreadsheet that one person updates.** It works. Build when it starts costing you mistakes or hours, not before.
- **A ready-made tool already fits, and you're happy paying for it per user.** Then it's the cheaper choice. Custom is for when the tools you've tried don't fit.
- **The process itself is still changing every month.** If you're still working out how orders or bookings should flow, write it down and run it by hand for a while. Software freezes a process; build it once the process has settled.
- **You want it because a competitor has one.** A web app needs a job to do every day. If you can't name the three screens you'd open each morning, it isn't time yet.

## Spreadsheet, ready-made tool, no-code or custom: which fits?

There are four real options, and each one is right for someone. Here's how they compare for a small business.

| | Spreadsheet | Ready-made tool | No-code builder | Custom web app |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Upfront cost | Free | Low | Low to medium | From $1,200 with me |
| Ongoing cost | Free | Monthly, per user | Monthly, often per user | Hosting, plus changes you ask for |
| Fits your workflow | You adapt | You adapt | Mostly | Exactly |
| Who can see what | Weak | Set by the tool | Limited by the platform | Rules you define, enforced in the database |
| Grows with you | Breaks with more people and data | Up to the tool's limits | Up to the platform's limits | Add screens as you need them |
| Best for | One person, simple data | A standard process the tool was built for | A quick internal tool, short term | A workflow that's yours and runs every day |

No-code builders sit in the middle. They're quick for a simple internal form or list, and a fair choice to test an idea. They get harder when you need custom rules about who sees what, connections to other services, or a design your customers will use. Then you're back to working around the platform.

Custom business software makes sense when the workflow is specific to you, it runs every day and the alternatives make you bend to fit them. A useful comparison is cost per year: a ready-made tool charges every month for every user, while a custom build is a one-time price plus hosting and the changes you ask for.

## What should the first version include?

The cheapest feature is the one you don't build yet. A good first version does the daily job well and leaves the rest for later, once you've used it.

| Build now | Leave for version two |
| --- | --- |
| Secure logins for the people who need them | Extra roles nobody needs on day one |
| The three or four screens you'd open every day | Screens for things that happen once a month |
| Your current data, imported once | Live syncs with every other tool you use |
| The few numbers you check daily, as simple charts | Custom exports and scheduled reports |
| One notification that saves the most chasing (for example, a new order email) | Notifications for every event |
| A layout that works on desktop and phone | A separate mobile app |

Launch that, use it for a few weeks, then add what you missed. Clients often find half of the "later" list was never needed, and the half they do need is clearer once they've used the first version.

Everything in the left column is part of my starting price: secure logins, screens designed around your workflow, the data you manage, charts for the numbers you track and a layout that works on desktop and mobile.

## How long does it take to build a web app?

A custom dashboard usually takes 2–4 weeks, and a custom web app 4–8 weeks. The range depends on three things more than anything else:

- **How clear the scope is on day one.** A written list of screens and roles keeps a project at the short end. A feature that changes halfway through is the most common reason a project runs long.
- **How clean the data is.** One tidy spreadsheet imports quickly. Three files that spell the same customer three ways need cleaning first.
- **How fast feedback comes back.** You test each part as it's ready. Quick answers keep the build moving.

You don't wait until the end to see anything. Screens are shared as they're built, so by launch day you've already used most of it.

## How is your data kept safe, and who can see what?

This is where a custom build beats a spreadsheet most clearly. In a shared file, anyone with the link can usually see and change everything. In a web app, every person logs in, and the database itself decides what they can read and change.

I build on Supabase, which gives each project a Postgres database, logins and file storage. Access rules are written once in the database with [Supabase row level security](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/postgres/row-level-security), so a staff member who only handles orders can't open the finance numbers, even if someone forgets to hide a button. A customer who logs in sees only their own orders.

## Who owns the code, and what does it cost to run?

Two questions every small business should settle before any build starts, with any developer.

**Ownership and handover.** Agree in writing who holds the code, the hosting account and the database account, and what a handover includes if you ever change developers. There's no single right answer; what matters is that both sides know it before work begins, not after.

**Running costs.** After launch you pay for:

- hosting and the database, billed monthly by the providers;
- your domain, if the app has its own address;
- any outside services it uses, such as an email sender or a payment provider;
- changes and new features you ask for, quoted separately;
- ongoing maintenance after the first 30 days, if you want someone on call.

Compare that with what you pay today in subscriptions and staff time, and you have the honest number for whether the build is worth it.
