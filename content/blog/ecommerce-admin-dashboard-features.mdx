The **ecommerce admin dashboard features** a small store needs fit on one screen of notes. On launch day, you need to add and edit products, set stock, see and update orders, and find any order fast when a customer asks. Sales, discounts and staff logins come next. Everything else depends on how you sell.

The core of a good store admin panel:

- **Products:** add, edit, hide, photos, price, sizes or variants, collections.
- **Stock:** quantities and sold-out, so nobody can order what you can't send.
- **Orders:** a list of new orders, each order's details, and a status you can change.
- **Search:** find an order by customer name, phone or order number.
- **Sales:** put a product or collection on sale, then end the sale.
- **Access:** your own login, and limited logins for staff.

Below is the full checklist, what to add when, and what it costs in a custom build.

## What should an ecommerce admin panel include?

An admin dashboard for an ecommerce store is the private side of the site where you run the business. Shoppers never see it. Here's the checklist I go through with store owners before I quote, grouped by the job each screen does.

**Products**

- [ ] Add a product with photos, price, description, sizes or variants and its collection.
- [ ] Edit, hide or delete a product without a developer.
- [ ] Reorder products and collections, so new arrivals show first.

**Stock**

- [ ] Set a quantity per product or per size.
- [ ] Mark an item sold out, and have the store stop selling it.

**Orders**

- [ ] A list of orders, newest first, with the status of each.
- [ ] Each order's items, address, payment method and the customer's message.
- [ ] Change the status as you work: new, packed, shipped.
- [ ] Find an order by the customer's name, phone or order number.

**Sales and promotions**

- [ ] Put a product or a whole collection on sale, with the old price crossed out.
- [ ] Add a banner for a campaign, and remove it when it ends.

**Team and access**

- [ ] Your own owner login.
- [ ] Staff logins that only see what their job needs.

That's a complete admin for most small stores. Reports and charts are useful, but a store can run for years on an order list and a sales total. If you need a fuller picture of the business (stock across channels, suppliers, staff), that's a [custom dashboard](/services/dashboards) of its own, not the store admin.

## What should an order management dashboard show?

The order management dashboard is where the daily work happens: every order passes through it.

A simple status chain covers most stores: **new → packed → shipped**, with **cancelled** for the orders that don't go out. Each change should be one click, and the list should show at a glance which orders still need packing.

The job owners forget to plan for is the message that starts "Where is my order?". It comes by WhatsApp, Instagram or email, usually with only a name or a phone number. The admin should find that order from either, show its status, and let you answer in seconds.

Two more things are worth asking for:

- **The customer's note** on the order screen, so a gift message or a delivery instruction isn't lost.
- **Orders from other channels.** If you also take orders on WhatsApp, a custom admin can hold them next to the web orders, so you have one list instead of two.

## Can I manage orders without Shopify?

Yes. A custom store keeps its products and orders in its own database, and the admin dashboard is part of the store, built for it. You manage everything there, without a store platform in between. I don't do Shopify work; the stores I build are custom Next.js stores, each with its own admin.

That doesn't mean a custom store has no running costs. You still pay for hosting, a domain and payments, and changes after the 30 days of free bug fixes are quoted separately. What you don't get is an admin shaped by someone else's idea of a store. Whether that trade is right for you is the question my [custom ecommerce website guide](/blog/custom-ecommerce-website-guide) answers in its Shopify comparison.

## Who on your team should have access to what?

Give each login only what its job needs. An example split for a small store:

- **Owner:** everything, including prices, discounts and staff logins.
- **Order packer:** the order list and status changes, no prices or products.
- **Product helper:** adding and editing products and photos, no orders or customer details.

In the stores I build, these limits are enforced in the database, not just hidden in the menu. Supabase calls this [Row Level Security](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/postgres/row-level-security): rules that decide which rows each login can read or change. It means a staff login can't reach customer details by going around the admin screens.
