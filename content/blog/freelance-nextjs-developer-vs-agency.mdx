In the **freelance Next.js developer vs agency** choice, a freelancer (or a small studio led by the developer) is the better fit for most defined projects: a business site, an online store, a dashboard or an AI chatbot. You pay less, and you talk to the person writing your code. A larger agency is worth its higher price when you need several skills at once, like brand design, copywriting and ads alongside development, or a team working in parallel.

The real question is simpler than freelancer vs agency for website work: **who writes my code, and who do I message when something breaks?**

## Freelance Next.js developer vs agency: side by side

| | Freelance developer | Small founder-led studio | Larger agency |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who you talk to | The developer | The founder, who also builds | An account or project manager |
| Who writes the code | The person you hired | Usually the founder, with team help | A developer you may never meet |
| Price | Lowest | Close to freelance | Highest (team, managers, overheads) |
| Skills | One person's range | Core build plus a few helpers | Design, copy, ads, development |
| If someone is ill | The project waits | Someone else can step in | Covered by the team |
| Best for | Defined sites, stores, fixes | Same, with a bit more cover | Big, multi-skill projects |

Most comparisons stop at the first and last columns. The middle one is where a lot of good small-business work happens, and it's the one I run, so I explain it further down.

## When should you hire a freelancer and when an agency?

Decide by what you're building, not by the label. Here's how I'd choose for the projects I quote most often, with my own starting prices and delivery times so you have a real reference.

| Project | Freelancer or small studio | Larger agency | My starting price |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Landing page or portfolio site | Best fit: one person can design and build it | Overkill | $400, 5–7 days |
| Ecommerce store with admin dashboard | Good fit if they've shipped stores before | Only with a full brand launch | $1,000, 2–3 weeks |
| Store with customer logins and orders | Good fit with clear written scope | Fits if you also need marketing | $2,000 |
| Custom dashboard for your team | Good fit: the builder needs to learn your workflow | Fits for large organisations | $1,200, 2–4 weeks |
| Custom web app | Fits for a first version | Fits when several developers must work in parallel | $2,500, 4–8 weeks |
| AI chatbot on an existing site | Best fit | Overkill | $350, 3–5 days |
| React site not showing on Google | Best fit: one specialist audit | Overkill | Audit $150, setup $500 |

So when someone asks me whether to hire freelancer or agency, I ask what else they need. A new brand, a campaign and a website at the same time points to an agency. A website on its own almost never does.

## Is it cheaper to hire a freelancer or an agency?

Usually a freelancer, and the reason is structure, not skill. An agency's price pays for the developer, but also for the account manager, the project manager, the office and the sales team. A freelancer's price is mostly their own time.

That gap is smaller with a small studio. The small agency vs freelancer cost difference is often minor, because a founder-led studio has little in between you and the person building.

Three things matter more than the label when you compare quotes:

- **Fixed price for a written scope.** Compare quotes for the same list of pages and features. An hourly quote with no list can't be compared with anything.
- **What's included after launch.** My projects include 2 free revision rounds and 30 days of free bug fixes; ongoing maintenance is quoted separately. Ask every bidder the same.
- **How you pay.** I work 50% upfront and 50% on delivery. Staged payments like this protect both sides, with either model.

A cheap quote that skips the admin dashboard, the mobile layout or the SEO basics isn't cheaper. It's a different project.

## Who owns the code with an agency?

Whatever the contract says. Agencies and freelancers both handle this in different ways: some hand everything over at the end, some keep the code and accounts and run the site for you. Neither is wrong, and there's no single answer to look for.

What matters is that it's written down before you pay anything. Agree on these, with either side:

- [ ] Who holds the code repository, and whether you get a copy at handover.
- [ ] Whose account the hosting runs on. Hosts like Vercel let a project [move between accounts](https://vercel.com/docs/projects/transferring-projects), so ask whether that's on the table.
- [ ] Who registered the domain and who can change its settings.
- [ ] Which logins you get on day one: the admin dashboard, the store, analytics.
- [ ] What a handover includes if you decide to move to another developer, and what it costs.

If an agency or a freelancer won't answer these in writing, that tells you more than any portfolio. My [guide to working with a remote web developer](/blog/working-with-a-remote-web-developer) covers the rest of the paperwork: payments, terms and handover.

## What should you ask either one before you sign?

The same questions work for a freelancer and an agency. Ask them on the first call and listen for specific answers:

1. **"Who will write the code, and can I talk to them?"** With an agency, the person on the sales call is often not the builder.
2. **"Can I see two or three live sites like mine?"** Live links, not screenshots. Open them on your phone.
3. **"What's in the price, page by page?"** A list of pages and features, a delivery time and what happens after launch.
4. **"Who do I message when something breaks, and how fast do you reply?"** A name, not a ticket queue you don't know about.
5. **"What happens if you're unavailable for a week?"** A freelancer should have a plan. A small studio should name who covers.
6. **"What do we agree about code and accounts?"** The checklist above, in writing.

The answers also show how someone explains things, which is how every update will read for the whole project. ## How to pick in five minutes

If you only remember one thing from this comparison, use this order:

1. Write down what you need: pages, features, and anything beyond the website itself (brand, copy, ads).
2. If it's only the website, store, dashboard or chatbot, shortlist freelancers and small studios.
3. If it's a website plus a brand launch or a marketing campaign, shortlist agencies.
4. Ask every shortlisted option the six questions above and compare fixed quotes for the same list.
5. Pick the one who answered most specifically, not the one with the biggest team page.
