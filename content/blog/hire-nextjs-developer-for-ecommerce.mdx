Before you hire next.js developer for ecommerce work, ask to see a live store they built and test it yourself: place an order up to the payment step, find a sold-out product, and watch them add a product in the admin panel. A store is mostly the parts visitors never see, so a good-looking portfolio tells you very little.

**The seven tests below need no coding knowledge:**

- Run an order through to the payment step.
- Find out what happens to a sold-out product.
- Use the filters on your phone.
- Look for real product text in the page source.
- Watch the admin panel in action.
- Check product page speed on mobile.
- Ask who owns the data and accounts.

I build custom Next.js stores with admin dashboards, so you can run every one of these on my work too.

## What to check before you hire a Next.js developer for ecommerce

A general web developer can make a store look right. A good Next.js ecommerce developer has dealt with what goes wrong after launch: a product selling out mid-checkout, a price changing while someone has it in their cart, an owner who needs to update fifty items on a Sunday night.

Ask for links to live stores, then run these tests.

1. **Run an order through to the payment step.** Add two products, change a quantity, remove one, then go to checkout on your phone. The totals should update correctly every time. If the developer can give you a test mode, complete the whole order and check what confirmation arrives.
2. **Find what happens to a sold-out product.** It should say so clearly and stop people from buying it. If a sold-out item can still be ordered, the owner ends up apologising to customers by hand.
3. **Use the filters on your phone.** Open a category, filter by size or price, and go back. Your filters should survive the back button, and the page shouldn't jump. On a large catalogue this is where most shoppers either find something or leave.
4. **Look for real product text in the page source.** Open a product page, view the source and search for the product's name and price. If they're missing, Google has to do extra work to read your catalogue. Ask whether the page has [Product structured data](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/structured-data/product), the labelled code that tells Google the price and whether it's in stock.
5. **Watch the admin panel in action.** Ask for a screen-share of someone adding a product with photos, changing a price, and marking an order as shipped. You'll do this every day for years, so it matters more than the homepage animation.
6. **Check product page speed on mobile.** Run a mobile Lighthouse test on a product page, not the home page. Product pages carry the heaviest images. Google's [Core Web Vitals](https://web.dev/articles/vitals) explain what the scores mean.
7. **Ask who owns the data and accounts.** The domain, hosting, database and payment accounts should be in your name. You should be able to export products and orders without asking anyone.

A developer who has built real stores will welcome these. One who hasn't will try to move the conversation back to design.

## What should an ecommerce developer know?

Next.js skills are the starting point. For a store, a Next.js online store developer also needs to understand:

- **Cart and checkout logic.** Totals, quantities, discount rules, delivery charges and what happens when something changes halfway through.
- **Stock.** Keeping quantities right when orders come in, and showing shoppers the truth.
- **Your database.** I set up Supabase on each store: login, a Postgres database, image storage and access rules, so a customer can only ever see their own orders.
- **Fast catalogue pages.** Pre-rendered pages that stay current when prices change, plus optimised images and lazy loading (loading images only as the shopper scrolls to them).
- **Product SEO.** Category pages Google can read, unique titles and descriptions for each product, and clean links.

Ask which of these they personally built. Many developers have worked on a team where someone else handled the database or the payment flow. On my stores I've been the sole frontend developer on each build, and I'll say which parts that covers.

## Do you need a headless ecommerce developer?

**Headless** means the storefront your customers see is separate from the system that holds products and orders, connected by an API (a defined way for two systems to talk). Agency guides push it hard, usually alongside five-figure budgets.

A headless ecommerce developer makes sense if you already run a commerce platform and want a faster, custom front end on top of it. For most small and mid-sized stores, one custom Next.js app with its own database and admin panel is simpler: fewer systems, fewer bills, one place to look when something breaks.

One limit you should know about: I don't do Shopify work, including themes, apps and headless Shopify fronts. If you're staying on Shopify, hire a Shopify specialist. If you're leaving it, or starting fresh, I'd [compare Next.js and WordPress for a business site](/blog/nextjs-vs-wordpress-for-business-website) first, because a custom store isn't right for everyone.

A custom store is probably the wrong call if you need to launch this week, you have no budget for someone to maintain it later, or your catalogue is small and rarely changes. A few well-photographed products and WhatsApp ordering can be enough to prove there's demand.

## Red flags to avoid when hiring for a store

- **No live store to open.** Screenshots and Dribbble shots don't take orders. Ask for the link.
- **Only theme experience.** Installing a theme is a real skill, but it isn't building a cart, stock logic and an admin panel.
- **No admin panel in the quote.** If it's "extra" or unclear, you'll be paying a developer every time a price changes.
- **Accounts in their name.** Your domain or database belonging to the developer means leaving them is hard.
- **The full amount upfront.** A split payment gives both sides a reason to finish.
- **Promises about rankings or sales.** Nobody can honestly guarantee either. They can guarantee the work: correct totals, readable pages, a store you can run yourself.
