If you want to hire a Next.js developer, look for three things before anything else: live sites you can test yourself, a clear understanding of SEO and speed, and someone who explains their plan in plain words. A nice portfolio page is not enough. Next.js is chosen for search traffic and fast pages, so the person you hire should be able to prove both on their own work.

I'm a Next.js developer and the founder of Devrex Digital, where my team has built websites and web apps for 45+ brands. This is the checklist I'd use if I were on your side of the table.

## When it makes sense to hire a Next.js developer

[Next.js](https://nextjs.org/docs) is a React framework. It adds the things plain React leaves out: pages rendered on the server or at build time, routing, metadata for search engines, image optimization and API routes.

It's a good fit when:

- You need **Google traffic** and your current React site doesn't rank.
- You want a **fast marketing site** or **ecommerce store** with custom design, not a theme.
- You need a **dashboard or web app** with logins, roles and real data.
- You want an **AI chatbot** or other features that need a backend.

If you only need a simple blog that you update yourself every day, a CMS might be enough. I compare the two in [Next.js vs WordPress for a business website](/blog/nextjs-vs-wordpress-for-business-website).

## The skills that matter (and the ones that don't)

A long list of logos on a CV says little. These are the skills that decide whether your project works.

### Must-have skills

- **React and TypeScript.** Next.js is React. TypeScript catches mistakes before your visitors do.
- **The App Router and server components.** This is how modern Next.js apps are built. Ask them to explain, in one minute, what runs on the server and what runs in the browser.
- **Technical SEO.** Titles and descriptions per page, canonical URLs, sitemap, robots.txt, structured data. If they can't explain these, your site may look great and still be invisible.
- **Performance.** They should know what Largest Contentful Paint is and how to make the first screen load fast with optimized images, fonts and less JavaScript. [web.dev's LCP guide](https://web.dev/articles/lcp) is a good reference for what that means.
- **Backend basics.** Forms, emails, a database, auth. Most business sites need at least a working contact form and lead emails.

### Nice to have

- **Animation (GSAP, Framer Motion) and 3D (Three.js)** if your brand needs a standout look.
- **Dashboard and data work** if you need charts, filters and exports.
- **AI integration** if you want a chatbot that answers from your own content.

### What matters less than you think

Years alone. Someone with fewer years who ships fast, measurable sites is a better hire than someone with a long CV and no live work you can test.

## How to check a portfolio in ten minutes

Ask for links to live projects. Then do this on two or three of them:

1. **Open the page source** (right-click → View page source). Real text in the HTML means the developer understands server rendering.
2. **Open a few pages and compare the browser tab titles.** Each page should have its own.
3. **Run Lighthouse** in Chrome DevTools on mobile, or use my [free SEO audit tool](/tools/free-seo-audit). Low SEO scores on a developer's own site are a warning sign.
4. **Test on your phone.** Tap the menu, fill a form, scroll the whole page. Anything broken now will be broken on your site too.
5. **Ask what they built personally.** Agency portfolios are team work. That's fine, but you should know who did what.

## Questions to ask before you hire

These questions quickly show who has done the work before:

- **"How will each page get its own title, description and social preview image?"** You want to hear about server-side metadata, not a plugin added later.
- **"What will my Lighthouse scores look like on mobile, and how will you measure them?"** A good developer measures before and after, and doesn't promise scores they haven't tested.
- **"Where will the site be hosted and who owns the accounts?"** You should own the domain, hosting and code repository.
- **"What happens after launch?"** Ask about bug fixes, updates and what maintenance costs.
- **"How do we communicate, and how often will I see progress?"** Weekly previews on a staging link are a good sign.

## Red flags to watch for

- **No live links**, only screenshots or Dribbble shots.
- **Guaranteed rankings** ("page one in 30 days"). Nobody controls Google.
- **Full payment upfront** with no milestones.
- **Templates sold as custom work.** Ask directly whether the design is built from scratch.
- **Vague quotes** without a list of pages and features.
- **No questions about your business.** A developer who doesn't ask who your customers are will build a generic site.

## What a good project process looks like

How a developer runs the project matters as much as their code. This is the process I follow, and it's a fair standard to expect from anyone you hire:

1. **A free call.** We talk about your business, your customers and what the site must do. No code yet.
2. **A written quote.** A list of pages and features, the price and the delivery time. If something isn't on the list, it isn't in the price, so there are no surprises later.
3. **50% upfront.** Work starts once the first half is paid.
4. **Previews while I build.** You get a private link to see progress and give feedback before launch, not after.
5. **Two revision rounds.** Changes to what we agreed are included twice.
6. **Launch and handover.** The site goes live on your domain, and you own the code and the accounts. The second 50% is paid on delivery.
7. **Thirty days of free bug fixes.** If something breaks after launch, I fix it.

Ask any developer you're considering to walk you through their version of this. If they can't describe it clearly, the project will probably be unclear too.

## Freelancer, agency or full-time hire?

- **Freelancer:** best for a defined project (a site, a dashboard, a chatbot). You talk to the person writing the code and pay for the project, not a salary.
- **Agency:** best when you need design, development, content and ads together for a long time.
- **Full-time developer:** best when you have a product that needs daily work for years.

Many of the businesses I work with start with one project, then come back for the next feature when they need it. That's usually the cheapest way to find out whether a developer is a good fit.
