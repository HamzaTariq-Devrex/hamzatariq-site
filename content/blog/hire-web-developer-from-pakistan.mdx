To **hire a web developer from Pakistan** safely, check three things before you pay anything: live sites they really built (and which part they did), a written quote with scope, price and timeline, and how they take payment and which of your working hours they'll be reachable. Get those right and the rest is the same as hiring anyone remote.

Here's the short checklist. The sections below explain each point.

- [ ] Two or three live sites they built, tested on your phone, with a clear answer on which part was theirs.
- [ ] A written quote: pages or screens, features, price, timeline, revision rounds and support after launch.
- [ ] Staged payment through a method that leaves a record, agreed before work starts.
- [ ] A daily window when they answer calls and messages inside your working day.
- [ ] Ownership and handover of the code and accounts agreed in writing.

I'm writing this from the other side of the deal: I'm a web developer based in Pakistan, working with clients abroad.

## Hire a web developer from Pakistan: what to look for

The skills that matter are the same as anywhere. What changes is how much you can check before the first call, because you can't meet in person. Look for these:

- **Live work you can open.** Not screenshots or design mockups: real sites with real addresses that you can use on your phone. A Pakistani web developer with a few years of client work should have several.
- **Clear written English.** You'll do most of the project in writing. Their first replies to you are the best sample you'll get. Are they clear, specific and do they answer what you asked?
- **Questions about your business.** A good developer asks who your customers are, what the site must do and what you already have before quoting. A price with no questions is a guess.
- **A fixed quote for a defined scope.** For a website, store or dashboard, ask for a fixed price for a written list of pages and features, not an open hourly arrangement.
- **A public code profile.** Not every client project can be shown, but a GitHub profile with real activity tells you they write code themselves.
- **The right stack for your project.** For a fast business site or store, ask what they build with and why. I build in React and Next.js; my guide on [what to look for when you hire a Next.js developer](/blog/hire-nextjs-developer) covers the skills to test for that stack.

## Is it a good idea to hire a developer from Pakistan?

For many small businesses, yes, if you go in with clear terms. The honest pros and cons:

| | What it means for you |
| --- | --- |
| Price | Fixed quotes are often lower than local agencies for the same scope |
| English | Widely used in business and tech in Pakistan, so written communication usually works well |
| Working hours | Pakistan is ahead of the UK, Europe and the US; you get progress overnight, but live overlap has to be planned |
| Paying | A step more than a domestic transfer; agree the method before work starts |
| Meeting in person | Not practical, so your checks happen online |
| Legal recourse | Harder across borders, so staged payments and written terms do the protecting |

The trade-off is simple. You save money and often get work done while you sleep. In return, you have to be more careful up front, because chasing a problem across borders is harder than with someone down the road. Every check in this post is about doing that care cheaply.

## How do you check the work is really theirs?

This is the check most buyers skip, and the one that matters most. Many developers in Pakistan, me included, work at or with an agency. A portfolio can mix sites they built alone, sites they worked on with a team, and sites the agency built without them. None of that is a problem if it's honest.

How to tell, without technical knowledge:

1. **Ask which part they built.** "Did you design it, build the front end, build the admin, or all of it?" A real builder answers in detail and without hesitation.
2. **Ask about one decision.** "Why does the product page work this way?" or "How does the owner add a new product?" Someone who built it will explain the reasons.
3. **Ask to see the admin side.** For a store or dashboard, ask for a screen-share of adding a product or changing an order. Sites without an admin are fine; stores without one are a warning.
4. **Test the live site on your phone.** Open three pages, try the menu, try the contact or order flow. Then run it through [PageSpeed Insights](https://pagespeed.web.dev/), Google's free speed test, to see how it scores on mobile.
5. **Check the dates.** Ask when each site was built. Work from years ago tells you less about what they'd build for you today.

## How do payments to Pakistan work?

Agree the payment method in writing before any work starts. It's a small question that avoids the most awkward conversation later.

The methods you'll hear about most:

- **Payoneer.** The developer sends you a payment request; you pay by card or bank transfer and get a receipt. I take Payoneer.
- **Direct bank transfer.** An international transfer to the developer's bank account in Pakistan. Slower, but simple and fully on record. I take this too.
- **Marketplace payments.** If you hire through a marketplace, it handles payment and holds the money until you approve the work, for a fee.

Sources online disagree about which consumer payment apps work in Pakistan and which don't, and that changes over time. Don't rely on what you've read: ask the developer how they receive money, and pay that way.

Three rules that apply whatever the method:

- Pay in **stages**, tied to delivered work. I use 50% to start and 50% on delivery.
- Pay to an account **in the developer's or their company's name**, never to a friend or relative.
- Keep **every receipt**, with the amount, date and what it was for.

## How much working-day overlap will you get?

Pakistan is ahead of the UK and Europe, and further ahead of the US. In practice that's a strength if it's planned: you write the feedback at the end of your day and wake up to the changes.

What to agree before you hire:

- **A daily window** when the developer answers messages and takes calls inside your working day. For UK and European clients this is usually easy. For US clients, it means the developer working into their evening, so ask whether they do.
- **One channel** for project messages, such as WhatsApp or email, so nothing gets lost across apps.
- **How often you'll see progress.** A link to the work in progress after each part is better than a big reveal at the end.

The pillar guide covers time zones in more detail. For an offshore developer in Pakistan, the main thing is to ask about hours before the project starts, not after the first missed call.

## Red flags when you outsource to Pakistan

Most of these apply to any remote hire, and the [warning signs in a React developer's proposal](/blog/red-flags-when-hiring-a-react-developer) apply wherever they're based. A few come up more often when you hire directly from abroad:

- **No live sites, only screenshots or templates.** Anyone can show a design. Ask for addresses you can open.
- **Vague answers about who built what.** If they can't say which part of a site was theirs, assume it wasn't.
- **A price before any questions.** It usually means a template, or a quote that will grow later.
- **Payment to someone else's account, or by gift card or crypto.** Pay the developer or their company, through a method that leaves a record.
- **Full payment upfront for a new client.** Staged payment protects both sides; asking for everything at once on a first project is a warning.
- **No written quote.** Scope, price, timeline and revisions must be in writing before work starts.
- **Never available in your hours.** If they can't find a time that suits you before you've paid, it won't get easier after.
