How much does a custom dashboard cost? With me, a custom dashboard starts from **$1,200** and usually takes 2–4 weeks. A custom web app, with its own users and workflows, starts from **$2,500** and takes 4–8 weeks. If what you need is an online store with an admin dashboard to manage products and orders, that starts from $1,000.

The final price depends on the features, and every project is quoted after a free call. Below is what each price includes, what pushes a quote up, and how to get an accurate one fast.

The same terms apply to all of them:

- **Revisions:** 2 free revision rounds, and you test each part as it's ready.
- **Support:** 30 days of free bug fixes after launch. Ongoing maintenance is quoted separately.

Most guides on dashboard development cost give ranges that run from a few thousand to six figures. That's because they mix three different products (my [guide to a custom web app for small business](/blog/custom-web-app-for-small-business) explains how to tell which one you need): a reporting dashboard for a large company's data team, an admin dashboard for running a small business day to day, and a full software product. The prices above are for the second and third kind, built for small and mid-sized businesses.

## Dashboard or web app: which one are you paying for?

This is the biggest price decision, so settle it before you ask for quotes.

| | Custom dashboard | Custom web app |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who logs in | You and your team | Your team and your customers or partners |
| Main job | See and manage your data in one place | Run a whole workflow, often with sign-ups |
| Typical screens | Orders, products, stock, customers, charts | Everything in a dashboard, plus customer accounts and their own screens |
| Starts from | $1,200 | $2,500 |

A rule of thumb: if only your staff will ever log in, you probably need a dashboard. If customers need their own accounts to book, order, upload or track something, it's a web app.

A booking or appointment system can be either one, depending on who books. I can build one as a dashboard or a web app, quoted by features; I haven't built one for a client yet, so I'd quote it fresh from your workflow rather than from a past project.

## What makes a dashboard more expensive?

The starting price covers secure logins, screens built around your workflow, the data you manage and the charts you track, working on desktop and mobile. These are the things that raise a quote above it:

- **More user roles.** One login for the owner is simple. A manager who sees everything, staff who only edit orders and a warehouse role that only sees stock means each screen has to check who's looking. I enforce that in the database itself with [Supabase row level security](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/database/postgres/row-level-security), so a hidden button can't be bypassed, and each extra role adds testing.
- **Messy or scattered data.** Bringing in one clean spreadsheet is quick. Merging three files that spell customer names differently, or data stuck in another tool, is real work before the first screen is built.
- **Connections to other services.** Sending emails, WhatsApp notifications, taking payments or syncing with another system each add a piece that has to be built and tested.
- **Reports beyond simple charts.** Totals and charts for the numbers you track are included. Custom exports, scheduled reports and calculations with many business rules take longer.
- **Customer-facing screens.** The moment customers log in, it's a web app: sign-up, password reset, their own data kept separate, and more screens to design.
- **Unclear scope.** The most expensive feature is one that changes halfway. A clear list on day one keeps the price close to the quote.

Things that usually don't raise the price much: the number of products or orders you store, a mobile-friendly layout (that's standard) and your brand colours.

## What's included, and what costs extra later?

Included in every quote:

- the free call and a written list of screens, roles and data before I start;
- design of the screens around your workflow;
- the build in React and Next.js with a real database;
- secure logins for you and your team;
- launch, a walkthrough of how to use it, and 30 days of free bug fixes.

What can cost extra later:

- **New features** you think of after launch, quoted separately when you ask for them.
- **Ongoing maintenance** after the first 30 days, if you want someone on call.
- **Hosting and database plans.** These are billed by the providers, not by me. A small business dashboard often fits on their starter plans; check their current pricing pages, because those change.

Before you start, agree in writing who holds the code, the hosting and the database accounts, and what a handover includes if you ever change developers. That's worth settling with any developer, whatever the answer is.

## Is a custom dashboard worth it over spreadsheets or a ready-made tool?

It's worth it when your team spends time every day copying data between files and chats, and nobody is sure which version is current. A dashboard gives everyone one place to see orders, stock or customers, and the same report stops being rebuilt by hand.

<MyTake>
I tell clients not to build a custom dashboard if a ready-made tool already fits the way they work and they're happy paying for it per user. Custom pays off when the tools you've tried make you change your process, charge for features you never use, or miss the one screen you need every day. And if your whole business still fits in one spreadsheet that one person updates, wait: build when the spreadsheet starts costing you mistakes or hours, not before.
</MyTake>

A useful comparison is cost per year, not the build price alone. A ready-made tool charges every month for every user, forever. A custom dashboard is a one-time build price plus hosting and any changes you ask for.

## How to get an accurate quote and keep the price down

The fastest way to an accurate admin dashboard price is to arrive at the free call with these ready:

- [ ] A list of the people who'll log in and what each one should see and change.
- [ ] The spreadsheets or tools your data lives in today, with a sample export.
- [ ] The three or four screens you'd open every day (for example orders, stock, customers).
- [ ] The numbers you track, and how you calculate them now.
- [ ] Any service it must connect to, such as email, WhatsApp or payments.
- [ ] What must be ready for launch, and what can wait for a second phase.

That last point saves the most money. Launch with the screens you need every day, use it for a few weeks, then add the rest. You'll often find half of the "later" list was never needed.
