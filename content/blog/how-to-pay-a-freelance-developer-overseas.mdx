Here's **how to pay a freelance developer overseas** safely: use a traceable method in the developer's own name (Payoneer or an international bank transfer), pay in USD or another agreed currency against a written quote, and split the price into stages tied to work you can check. A deposit to start is normal; the rest should follow delivery, not a date. Before each payment, have the invoice, the agreed work for that stage, and the fee option settled in writing.

The safe payment in five points:

1. **A written quote** with scope, price, currency and payment stages.
2. **A traceable method** you can keep a receipt for.
3. **An account in the developer's name** or their company's name.
4. **Stages tied to work**, such as 50% to start and 50% on delivery.
5. **Fees agreed upfront**, so the developer receives the invoiced amount.

## What is the safest way to pay a foreign freelancer?

The safest method is the one that leaves a record on both sides, reaches the developer in their country, and lets you check who you're paying. Most guides compare transfer apps by fees. For a single website project, three things matter more than the fee.

**Can you trace it?** A transfer with a receipt showing the amount, date, sender and receiver protects you if there's ever a dispute. Cash apps between friends, gift cards and crypto don't.

**Does the name match?** The receiving account should be in the name on the quote, or the developer's registered company. A different name needs a clear reason before you send anything.

**Can they actually receive it?** Payment services work differently from country to country. Don't pick a method from an article; ask the developer how they get paid, then pay that way.

Here are the common freelancer payment methods for a one-off project:

| Method | Good for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Payoneer | Paying a request by card or bank, with a receipt | Check the request comes from the developer's own account |
| International bank transfer | Larger amounts, a clear bank record | Slower; agree who pays the fees |
| Marketplace with escrow | Hiring someone you met on that marketplace | The marketplace takes a fee from the project |
| Gift cards, crypto, cash apps | Nothing in business | Hard or impossible to trace or dispute |

## Should I pay a developer upfront?

Yes, part of it. A deposit is how a freelance developer books time for your project and turns away other work. Asking for nothing upfront is unusual; asking for everything upfront is a warning sign.

The split I use on every project is 50% upfront and 50% on delivery. The first half starts the work. The second half is due when the agreed scope is delivered, so you're never paying for work you haven't seen.

What makes an upfront payment safe isn't the percentage, it's what you have before you send it:

- A written quote that lists the pages and features, and what's not included.
- A start date and a delivery time. With me, a landing page takes 5–7 days and an ecommerce store with admin dashboard 2–3 weeks.
- An invoice that matches the quote.
- Proof the developer is real: live sites you can open and reviews you can trace to a business. My guide on [hiring a web developer from Pakistan](/blog/hire-web-developer-from-pakistan) explains how to check that the work is really theirs.

## How do milestone payments work on a website project?

**Milestone payments** split the price into stages, and each stage is paid when a defined piece of work is done. The key word is defined. "Halfway through" isn't a milestone; "the store is live with products and checkout working" is.

Here's what that looks like on my real packages, paid 50/50:

| Package (from) | Upfront payment | On delivery | What you should have before the second payment |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ecommerce store with admin dashboard ($1,000) | $500 | $500 | The store working end to end: products, cart, checkout, and the admin dashboard to manage orders |
| Full ecommerce app ($2,000) | $1,000 | $1,000 | Customer logins, accounts and orders working, plus the admin side |

On a larger build, some developers add a middle milestone, such as approved designs or a working first section. Whatever the split, each stage should name the work that triggers it and how you'll check it: a preview link you can click through, not a screenshot.

## Who pays the transfer fees on an international payment?

Whoever you agree, in writing, before the first invoice. Nobody else's guide makes this obvious, but it's the most common small argument on international projects: the client sends the invoiced amount, a bank takes its cut on the way, and the developer receives less.

International bank transfers usually give the sender a choice:

- **Sender pays all fees.** The developer gets the full amount. Simplest for a fixed price.
- **Fees shared.** Each side pays its own bank. The developer may still receive slightly less if a bank in the middle takes a fee.
- **Receiver pays.** The fees come out of the amount the developer gets.

My advice for anyone who needs to pay an international freelancer: agree that the developer receives the full invoiced amount, and pick the option that makes that true. Payoneer works differently from a bank transfer, so ask the developer how its fees fall on a payment request before you pay.

## What should an invoice from an overseas developer show?

An invoice is your record if anything goes wrong, and your accountant will want it. It should show:

- The developer's name or company name, matching the quote and the receiving account.
- Your business name.
- The project, and which stage this payment covers ("50% upfront, ecommerce store").
- The amount and the currency.
- How to pay, with the account details.
- The date, and an invoice number if the developer uses them.

Ask your accountant which tax paperwork, if any, your country needs when you pay someone abroad. It varies by country, and a payment company's blog isn't the place to settle it.

## What happens to the final payment if something needs fixing?

This is where projects go sour, so settle it in the quote. Three things to agree:

1. **Revisions before delivery.** With me, that's 2 free revision rounds. Changes inside the agreed scope are part of the price.
2. **What counts as delivered.** The agreed pages and features working, not "perfect". A new idea after delivery is new work, quoted separately.
3. **Support after the final payment.** I include 30 days of free bug fixes after launch, so a bug found after you've paid still gets fixed. Ongoing maintenance is quoted separately.

Also keep the running costs apart from the project price. Hosting, the database plan and the domain are billed by those providers, usually monthly or yearly, not by the developer. On the Next.js builds I do that's typically hosting on Vercel and a database on Supabase, each with its own plans ([Vercel pricing](https://vercel.com/pricing), [Supabase pricing](https://supabase.com/pricing)). Agree in writing who holds each account, who pays each bill and what a handover includes.

## How to pay a freelance developer overseas: the checklist before you send

Before you press send on any payment, run through this:

- [ ] The amount matches the invoice, and the invoice matches the quote.
- [ ] The receiving name matches the developer or their company.
- [ ] If the bank details changed since the last payment, I confirmed it with the developer on a different channel, such as a call or WhatsApp.
- [ ] The fee option means the developer receives the full amount.
- [ ] I've seen the work this payment is for, on a link I can click through.
- [ ] I've saved the invoice and the receipt together.
