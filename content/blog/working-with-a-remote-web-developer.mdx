Working with a remote web developer is safe and often good value when three things are in place: written terms, staged payments, and every account in your own name. Agree the scope, price, timeline and revisions before work starts. Pay part upfront and the rest on delivery, through a method that leaves a record. Keep a short daily window for calls and do the rest in writing, so time zones work for you instead of against you.

I'm on the other side of these calls. I'm based in Pakistan, work with clients worldwide, and run every project this way.

**Before any remote project starts, you should have:**

1. A written scope: pages, features and what's not included.
2. A price split into stages, such as 50% upfront and 50% on delivery.
3. A traceable payment method the developer can receive.
4. The domain, hosting and database registered in your name.
5. An agreed way and time to talk, and how often you'll see progress.

Everything below explains why each of these matters and what good looks like.

## What does working with a remote web developer look like?

Day to day, it looks a lot like working with a developer in your own city, minus the office visits. You talk on a call at the start, agree what's being built, then most of the work happens in writing: messages, shared links to the work in progress, and short calls when something needs a decision.

A typical project with me runs like this:

- **A free call.** We talk through your goals, the pages you need and the features that matter. You get a clear written quote afterwards.
- **Plan and design.** I map the pages and the path visitors take, and design around your brand before any code is written.
- **Build, with reviews as each part is ready.** You don't wait until the end to see anything. You get a link to the work in progress and give feedback on it.
- **Launch and SEO setup.** The site goes live with titles, descriptions, a sitemap and structured data in place.
- **Support.** 30 days of free bug fixes after launch. Ongoing maintenance is quoted separately.

None of that needs you in the same room. What it needs is clear writing on both sides, and that's the skill to look for when you hire a remote developer. ## Is it safe to hire a remote developer?

Yes, when you protect two things: your money and your accounts. Most horror stories come down to one of these:

- **Paying everything upfront** to someone who then goes quiet.
- **The developer owning the domain or hosting**, so the site is effectively theirs.
- **No written scope**, so "finished" means something different to each side.
- **Hiring from a portfolio of screenshots** that turned out not to be their work.

Each one has a simple guard:

| Risk | Guard |
| --- | --- |
| Paying for work you never get | Staged payments: part upfront, the rest on delivery |
| Losing control of your site | Domain, hosting, database and payment accounts in your name from day one |
| Arguing about what was agreed | A written scope with pages, features and revision rounds |
| A fake or borrowed portfolio | Live sites you can open and test, and reviews you can trace back to the client |
| Disappearing after launch | A defined support period, written into the terms |

With those in place, the risk of hiring an offshore web developer is about the same as hiring one locally. A developer in your own city can also take a full deposit and vanish. Distance changes how you check someone, not whether you can.

## How should you brief a remote developer?

A good brief does more for a remote project than any tool. When you can't point at a screen together, the written brief is what both of you come back to.

Send these before the free call, or right after it:

- **What the site is for.** One or two sentences: "Take orders for our handmade bags" is more useful than "a modern website".
- **The pages you need.** A simple list is enough: home, shop, about, contact, policies.
- **Sites you like, and why.** Two or three links with a line each ("I like how the menu works here") tell a designer more than a page of adjectives.
- **What you already have.** Logo, photos, text, an old site, a domain. Say what's missing too, so it can be planned for.
- **Who decides.** If three people will give feedback, name the one who makes the final call. Remote projects stall most often when feedback comes from several people who disagree.
- **Any fixed date.** A launch, a sale or an event. Say it upfront so the plan can work back from it.

You don't need to write in technical terms. Plain sentences in your own words are exactly what a good developer wants. ## How do time zones work with a remote developer?

Time zones are less of a problem than most owners expect, and sometimes a help.

What works is a split between **live time** and **written time**:

- **Live time** is a short window when you're both online. Use it for calls, quick decisions and anything that would take five messages to settle.
- **Written time** is everything else. You send feedback at the end of your day, the developer works on it during theirs, and you wake up to progress.

I'm available in US, UK and EU time zones, which in practice means I book calls inside your working day and answer messages during your morning or afternoon, not just mine.

Three habits make the gap work:

- **Batch your feedback.** One list per review, with screenshots, beats a stream of single messages through the day.
- **Say what's blocking.** "I need the logo before the header can be finished" saves a full day of waiting.
- **Agree a response time.** For example, messages answered within your working day. Knowing when to expect a reply removes most of the stress.

## How do I pay a developer in another country?

This is the question I get most from clients abroad. The answer is a method that leaves a record on both sides, that the developer can actually receive in their country, and that you're comfortable using.

I take **Payoneer** and **bank transfer** for international clients. Both give you a receipt, show the amount and date, and are normal for businesses paying a remote freelance developer.

What to agree before the first payment:

- **The currency.** My prices are in USD, so the quote and the invoices match.
- **How the price is split.** My terms are the same on every project: 50% upfront and 50% on delivery.
- **What triggers each payment.** For the second half, that's delivery of the agreed scope, not a date on the calendar.
- **Who covers transfer fees.** Agree it upfront so neither side is short.

What to avoid:

- Paying the full amount before any work exists.
- Gift cards, or any method you can't trace or dispute.
- Paying a personal account under a different name from the one on the quote, without a clear reason.

## What should be in the agreement with a remote developer?

You don't need a long legal document for a website project. You need a written agreement both of you can point to. Mine covers, and yours should cover:

- **Scope:** the pages and features included, and what's not. For example, a store package with an admin dashboard, up to 10 pages and unlimited products (my [custom ecommerce website guide](/blog/custom-ecommerce-website-guide) shows what that covers).
- **Price and payment stages:** the total, and when each part is due.
- **Timeline:** when work starts and the expected delivery, plus what you need to provide and by when.
- **Revisions:** how many rounds are included. I include 2 free revision rounds.
- **After launch:** what support is included. With me, 30 days of free bug fixes; maintenance after that is quoted separately.
- **Ownership:** that you own the code, the content and every account once the work is paid for.
- **Extras:** how anything outside the scope is priced. For example, my extra pages are $50 each.

If something isn't in writing, assume it isn't included. A good remote developer will be glad you asked, because a clear scope protects them too.

## What happens if something goes wrong mid-project?

Even well-run projects hit bumps. What matters is that the terms already say how to handle them, so nobody has to argue from memory.

**A deadline slips.** Find out why first. Often the developer is waiting on content: photos, product details or page text. If the delay is on their side, a clear written timeline gives you something specific to point to, and the staged payment means the second half isn't due until the work is delivered.

**You disagree about what was included.** Go back to the written scope. If a feature isn't on it, it's an extra and should be quoted as one. If it is on it, it's part of the price. This is the single best reason to write the scope down before work starts.

**You want changes after a review.** That's what revision rounds are for. Gather everyone's feedback into one list per round, so each round counts.

**The developer goes quiet.** Send one clear written message asking for a status update and a date. Because the domain, hosting and code are already in your name, you can hand the project to someone else if you have to, without losing what's been built.

That last point is why ownership matters so much in remote work. It turns a worst case from "start again" into "find another developer".

## Will language and communication be a problem?

It shouldn't be, and you can test it before you hire. Read how the developer writes in their first few messages: are the answers clear, specific and on topic? That's how every update will read for the whole project.

I work in English (professional working proficiency) and Urdu (native). After every call I send a short written summary of what we agreed and what happens next, so nothing depends on anyone's memory or on catching every word of a call with a poor connection.

Two habits help on your side too: put decisions in writing even if you made them on a call, and ask when something isn't clear. A good remote developer would rather answer a question than rebuild a page.

## Freelancer, offshore agency or platform: which way should you outsource web development?

There are three common ways to outsource web development to someone abroad. Each suits a different owner.

| Option | What you get | Good for | Watch out for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Remote freelancer, hired directly | One person who designs, builds and supports the site | Clear projects where you want to talk to the person doing the work | One person's availability; check their support terms |
| Offshore agency | A team with a project manager in between | Large builds with several specialists | You may never speak to the developer; layers add cost |
| Freelance platform | A marketplace that holds payments and shows reviews | First-time hires who want the platform's payment protection | Platform fees on both sides; reviews can be thin |

I work directly, as the person who builds your site, with my team at Devrex Digital behind me when a project needs it. That's the setup I'd look for in most small and mid-sized projects: you talk to the person writing the code, and there's no middle layer to pay for.

## Warning signs of a bad remote hire

Most bad hires show warning signs before any money moves. Walk away, or at least slow down, if you see these:

- **No live sites.** Only screenshots or mockups, and no link you can open and click through.
- **Reviews you can't trace.** Testimonials with no business name, or businesses you can't find.
- **Everything upfront.** A request for the full amount before work starts.
- **They want to own your accounts.** "I'll register the domain for you" in their own name, or hosting you can't log into.
- **Vague scope.** A price with no list of pages and features behind it.
- **Promises nobody can keep.** Guaranteed rankings, guaranteed sales, or a full store delivered in a day.
- **Pressure to decide now.** A discount that expires tonight is a sales tactic, not a reason to hire.

None of these depend on the country. They're the same signs I'd look for if I were hiring someone myself.

## What should you own, and how should handover work?

Ownership is the most important thing to settle when you work with someone remote, because you can't walk into their office if things go wrong. Put each of these in your name from the start:

- **The domain**, registered with your email address.
- **The hosting account.** On the Next.js sites I build, that's usually Vercel. If a developer starts the project under their own account, Vercel supports [transferring a project](https://vercel.com/docs/projects/transferring-projects) to yours.
- **The database.** For my builds, a Supabase project. Supabase also allows [transferring a project](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/platform/project-transfer) between organisations, so it can move to you without a rebuild.
- **The code**, in a repository you own or can download.
- **Payment, email and analytics accounts**, such as your payment provider, email sending service and Google Search Console.

At handover, you should receive admin access to all of the above, a short guide to the admin dashboard if there is one, and a list of any paid services the site uses. You should be able to switch developers next month without asking anyone for permission.

Use this before you pay any remote developer, including me:

- [ ] I've opened and clicked through at least one live site they built.
- [ ] I've found at least one review I can trace back to a real business.
- [ ] I have a written scope with pages, features and what's excluded.

- [ ] The domain, hosting and database will be in my name.
- [ ] I know when we'll talk and when I'll see progress.
